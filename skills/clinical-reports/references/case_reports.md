# Case Reports (CARE Guideline)

A case report describes an unusual presentation, a novel diagnosis, an
unexpected complication, or an instructive management course in a single patient
(or small series). Its value is educational: it contributes an observation that
the aggregate literature has not yet captured. Write it to the **CARE (CAse
REport) guideline**, a 13-item checklist that mirrors what editors and readers
expect.

## CARE Checklist (13 items)

1. **Title** — include the words "case report" (or "case study") and the area of
   focus. Example: "Unusual Presentation of Acute Myocardial Infarction in a
   Young Adult: A Case Report".
2. **Keywords** — 2–5 terms; prefer MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) for indexing.
3. **Abstract** — structured or unstructured, ~150–250 words: what is novel;
   patient's primary concerns and key history; diagnoses; interventions;
   outcomes; the take-home lesson.
4. **Introduction** — brief background on the condition, why this case matters,
   and a short pointer to similar cases in the literature.
5. **Patient information** — de-identified demographics, relevant medical,
   family, and social history, and comorbidities. Record informed consent for
   publication (see below).
6. **Clinical findings** — chief complaint, presenting symptoms, and pertinent
   physical-exam findings.
7. **Timeline** — the key events (symptoms → diagnosis → intervention →
   outcome) as a dated table or figure. A figure often reads best; generate it
   with `scientific-schematics`.
8. **Diagnostic assessment** — tests performed, results and interpretation, the
   differential considered, the rationale for the final diagnosis, and any
   diagnostic challenges.
9. **Therapeutic intervention** — drugs (name, dose, route, duration),
   procedures, and the reasoning behind the choices, including alternatives.
10. **Follow-up and outcomes** — clinician-assessed outcome, follow-up duration,
    patient-reported outcomes, and adherence. State long-term outcome if known.
11. **Discussion** — strengths and novelty; comparison to existing literature;
    limitations of a single-case observation; plausible mechanisms; clinical
    implications and lessons; open questions.
12. **Patient perspective** — the patient's own experience, when they can share
    it (encouraged, not required).
13. **Informed consent** — a statement that written consent for publication was
    obtained (see language below).

## Timeline format (example)

```
Day 0    Presented with symptoms X, Y, Z
Day 2    Test A performed → finding B
Day 5    Started drug C at <dose/route>
Day 14   Clinical improvement noted
Month 3  Follow-up: complete resolution
```

## Informed consent

Publication of a case report requires the patient's (or a proxy's) written
informed consent, distinct from consent to treatment. Most journals require an
explicit statement and may ask for the signed form.

- Standard language: *"Written informed consent was obtained from the patient
  for publication of this case report and any accompanying images. A copy of the
  written consent is available for review by the Editor-in-Chief of this
  journal."*
- **Deceased or unable to consent** — describe the proxy/next-of-kin consent
  obtained.
- **Pediatric** — parental or guardian consent (and assent where appropriate).
- Consent cannot fully substitute for de-identification: minimize identifiers
  even with consent on file.

## De-identification for publication

Follow HIPAA Safe Harbor: remove or alter all 18 identifiers and aggregate ages
over 89 to "90 or older". Practical habits:

- Use "the patient", not names.
- Report an age band ("a woman in her 60s") unless the exact age is clinically
  load-bearing.
- Use relative time ("3 months prior"), not calendar dates.
- Drop institution names unless essential.
- Crop/blur identifying features in images; a full-face photo is itself an
  identifier and needs specific consent.

## Journal-specific requirements

Read the target journal's Instructions for Authors before drafting; typical
constraints:

- Word count (commonly 1,500–3,000 words).
- Figure/table caps and image resolution.
- Reference style (AMA, Vancouver, APA) — manage with `citation-management`.
- Structured vs unstructured abstract.
- CARE checklist submission (many journals require it) and a completed
  consent/permission form.
